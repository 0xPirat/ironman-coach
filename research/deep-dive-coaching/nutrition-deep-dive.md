# Nutrition Deep Dive — Periodized Daily Fueling, Carb-Loading, Gut Training, Supplements & RED-S

**Purpose:** The methodology file (`ironman-training-methodology.md`, §9) covers **race-day/in-session** carbohydrate, hydration, and "train the gut" at a high level. This file goes deeper on the parts it does NOT cover: **daily nutrition periodized across training phases**, the **race-week carb-load protocol**, granular **gut-training progressions**, **sweat/sodium testing methodology**, **supplement evidence quality**, **body-composition management**, and **RED-S / under-fueling warning signs**.

> Evidence note: Daily-intake and supplement guidance here rests on reasonably good sport-nutrition science (position stands, IOC consensus). Ironman-specific carb-loading amounts and gut-training rates are **coaching-practice extrapolations** from marathon/cycling research — flagged where relevant, consistent with the existing file's honesty.

## 1. Periodized Daily Nutrition ("Fuel for the Work Required")

The key concept beyond race fueling: **daily carbohydrate should track daily training demand**, not be a fixed number. Protein and fats stay relatively steady; **carbs are the dial** ([TrainingPeaks — periodized carb intake](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/carbohydrate-intake-training-racing/), [GSSI — dietary carbohydrate](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/dietary-carbohydrate-and-the-endurance-athlete-contemporary-perspectives), [Simple Endurance — fuel for the work required](https://simpleendurance.medium.com/fuel-for-the-work-required-a-periodized-nutrition-approach-for-everyday-endurance-athletes-7d8ad6844a3b)).

### 1.1 Daily carbohydrate targets by day type

| Day type | Carbohydrate | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Rest / very light** | **3–5 g/kg/day** | Recovery day, technique swim only |
| **Moderate** | **5–7 g/kg/day** | Two ~1 h aerobic sessions, or one quality session |
| **Heavy / long** | **6–10 g/kg/day** | Long ride, long run, big brick, back-to-back weekend |
| **Very heavy (peak block, camp)** | **8–12 g/kg/day** | Multiple long sessions/day |

(Protein steady at **~1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** to support repair/adaptation; fats fill the rest of energy needs.) Sources: [Simple Endurance](https://simpleendurance.medium.com/fuel-for-the-work-required-a-periodized-nutrition-approach-for-everyday-endurance-athletes-7d8ad6844a3b), [NSCA — carbohydrate periodization](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/ptq/carbohydrate-periodizationpart-1-fueling-exercise/), [The Sports Foodie](https://www.thesportsfoodie.com/what-is-nutrition-periodization-for-athletes/).

### 1.2 How it maps to training phases

| Phase | Daily-carb emphasis | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Base** | Lower average (3–6 g/kg most days); some *deliberate* low-carb easy sessions | "Train-low" occasionally to build fat oxidation / mitochondrial signaling — but NEVER on key or long days |
| **Build** | Higher on the more frequent quality/long days (6–8 g/kg) | Race-specific fueling starts to be rehearsed in long sessions |
| **Peak** | High on the big sessions (8–10 g/kg); full race-nutrition rehearsal | Practice race-day intake rates (see methodology §9) |
| **Taper / race week** | Maintain, then carb-load (see §2); volume drops so total energy drops, but glycogen fills | Don't over-restrict just because training is down — under-fueling the taper blunts freshness |

**"Train-low" caveat (evidence quality: moderate, contested):** periodically doing *easy, short* sessions with low carbohydrate availability may enhance fat-oxidation and aerobic adaptation, but the performance benefit is unproven and the risks (poor quality, immune suppression, RED-S) are real. Use sparingly, never for key/long/quality sessions, and never for athletes with any under-fueling risk ([GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/dietary-carbohydrate-and-the-endurance-athlete-contemporary-perspectives), [TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/carbohydrate-intake-training-racing/)).

**AI-coach implication:** compute a daily carb target from the day's planned TSS/duration; flag long/key days as "fuel high"; only ever suggest low-carb on genuinely easy short days, and suppress that suggestion entirely if any RED-S flag exists.

## 2. Race-Week Carbohydrate Loading (Full-Distance Specific)

Modern carb-loading is **not** the old week-long depletion/loading swing. It's a short, high-carb window at the end of the taper.

### 2.1 The protocol

- **Amount:** **10–12 g carbohydrate per kg body mass per day** during the loading window; **8–10 g/kg/day** is a slightly lower, often more practical target that still maximally fills muscle glycogen ([NutritionTriathlon — carb loading](https://www.nutritiontriathlon.com/blog/how-to-carb-load-for-an-ironman-triathlon), [Precise Multisport](https://www.precisemultisport.com/blogs/carb-loading-for-ironman-triathlon), [LaPrimaFit](https://www.laprimafit.com/en/nutrition-strategy-for-ironman-carb-loading)).
  - For a **70 kg athlete:** ~**700–840 g/day** (at 10–12 g/kg).
- **Window:** begin **36–48 h before race start** (i.e., roughly the two days before). Many athletes ramp carb-rich food across the whole final week during taper.
- **Practical execution:** shift to **low-fibre, low-fat, carb-dense** foods (white rice, pasta, potatoes, bread, sports drinks, bananas, honey) — high fibre makes 10 g/kg physically hard and risks GI issues. Spread across many small meals.
- **Expect weight gain:** each gram of stored glycogen holds ~3–4 g water, so glycogen supercompensation adds **~1.5–2 kg of "water weight"** — this is normal and beneficial (it's fuel + hydration), not fat ([NutritionTriathlon](https://www.nutritiontriathlon.com/blog/how-to-carb-load-for-an-ironman-triathlon), [ScienceInsights](https://scienceinsights.org/what-to-eat-the-day-before-a-triathlon-carbs-timing/)).

### 2.2 Race morning (adjunct to methodology §9)

- Pre-race breakfast **3–4 h out: ~1–4 g/kg** of easily digested carbs (familiar, low-fibre, low-fat), then top-ups until the swim. Nothing untried on race day.

**AI-coach implication:** in race week, generate a day-by-day carb-loading plan in grams from the athlete's body mass, warn about the expected/normal scale weight gain in advance (so it doesn't spook them), and stress "nothing new."

## 3. Gut Training — Granular Progression

The gut is trainable: repeated high-carb feeding upregulates intestinal transporters (SGLT1 for glucose, GLUT5 for fructose), raising tolerance and absorption ([NEVERSECOND — IM fuel guide](https://never2.com/blogs/guides/ironman-fuel-and-hydration-guide), methodology §9).

### 3.1 A concrete multi-week progression

| Week block | Long-session intake target | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Start (Base/early Build) | **~40–50 g carb/h** | Establish tolerance; simple single/dual-source |
| +2–4 weeks | **+5–10 g/h per progression step → ~60 g/h** | Introduce glucose:fructose ~2:1 products |
| Build | **~70–80 g/h** | Match target race products exactly (gels/drink/bars you'll race) |
| Peak | **race target (bike 70–90+ g/h, run 50–80 g/h)** | Full rehearsal at race intensity, heat, and duration |

### 3.2 Practical gut-training rules

- **Rehearse on the sessions that mirror the race:** long rides and big bricks at race intensity — GI tolerance is intensity- and posture-dependent (running tolerance < cycling).
- **Practice the exact products/brands** you'll race; switching brands on race day is a classic error.
- **Dual-source (glucose:fructose ~2:1)** is what enables >60–70 g/h without GI distress.
- **Fluid + sodium gate carbohydrate absorption** — train them together (see §4).
- If GI distress appears, **step back one level**, hold, then progress again — same logic as return-to-running progressions.

> Evidence quality: the *principle* of gut trainability is well supported; the specific weekly +5–10 g/h rate is coaching-practice convention, not a validated schedule.

## 4. Sweat-Rate & Sodium Testing Methodology

Personalized hydration/sodium beats generic guidance, especially for a long, potentially hot race.

### 4.1 Sweat rate (do-it-yourself, accurate)

The gold-standard practical method is **body-mass change** ([CTS — sweat test](https://trainright.com/sweat-test-for-athletes/), [PMC — sweat methodology review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5371639/)):

1. Weigh **nude, dry, pre-session**.
2. Do a **~60 min** session at target intensity/conditions; **record any fluid drunk** (weigh/measure it).
3. Towel off, weigh **nude post-session**.
4. **Sweat loss (L) = (pre-weight − post-weight in kg) + fluid consumed (kg/L).** Divide by hours → **sweat rate (L/h)**. (1 kg ≈ 1 L.)

Repeat across conditions (cool vs hot) — sweat rate can vary hugely (commonly ~0.5–2.5 L/h). Test in conditions representative of the target race.

### 4.2 Sweat sodium concentration

More involved; options by accuracy/cost:

- **Lab / clinic sweat test** (regional absorbent patch, analyzed): most reliable one-off number ([HORIBA](https://www.horiba.com/usa/water-quality/applications/human-health-care/measuring-sodium-and-potassium-concentrations-in-athletes-sweat/), [Precision — sweat testing](https://www.precisionhydration.com/)).
- **Portable sodium meter** (e.g., LAQUAtwin) on collected sweat: fast, cheaper, decent.
- **Wearable sweat sensors:** measure during exercise; convenient but accuracy varies.
- **Proxy cue:** heavy visible salt crusting / white marks on kit and gritty/salty taste suggests a "salty sweater" who needs more sodium.

Sweat sodium varies widely (~200 to >1500 mg/L). Note variability sources: patch site, timing, skin cleaning, storage, analysis method — one test is a guide, not gospel ([PMC — methodology & variability](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5371639/), [J Sports Sci — normative data by sport](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2019.1633159)).

### 4.3 Turning it into a plan

- Replace a practical fraction of sweat loss during the race (full replacement is rarely possible/desirable) and add sodium roughly matched to concentration × sweat rate.
- **Rehearse the hydration/sodium plan on long sessions**, same as fuel.

**AI-coach implication:** build a guided sweat-test workflow (pre/post weights + fluid intake in) that computes L/h per session and per condition; store a sodium estimate (test-derived or "salty sweater" self-report); fold both into race-day hydration targets and heat adjustments.

## 5. Supplements — With Honest Evidence Quality

The 2018 **IOC consensus statement** identifies **five** supplements with genuinely compelling performance evidence: **caffeine, creatine, beta-alanine, sodium bicarbonate, dietary nitrate**. Everything else is largely unproven or health- (not performance-) oriented ([IOC consensus — Humankinetics](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/28/2/article-p104.xml), [BeetrootPro summary](https://beetrootpro.com/blog/ioc-recognized-supplements-for-endurance-athletes)).

| Supplement | Evidence quality (endurance) | Relevance to a full-distance athlete |
|---|---|---|
| **Caffeine** | **Strong.** Improves endurance capacity, TT performance, lowers perceived exertion, aids cognition under fatigue | **High.** ~3–6 mg/kg pre-race and/or strategic late-race top-ups; rehearse in training; watch GI/jitters/sleep. Well worth using. |
| **Dietary nitrate (beetroot)** | **Good**, strongest for shorter/higher-intensity efforts (<40 min TTs, type-II fibre function); reduces O₂ cost / improves economy | **Modest** for a very long, sub-threshold event; benefit likely smaller than in short events. Cheap, low-risk; worth a trial. Load ~2–3 days pre-race. |
| **Beta-alanine** | **Modest**, best for 1–4 min high-intensity/repeated efforts (buffers via carnosine) | **Low** for steady Ironman intensity; not a priority for a long aerobic race. |
| **Sodium bicarbonate** | **Good** for high-intensity buffering | **Low** for Ironman pacing; GI risk high. Skip. |
| **Creatine** | **Strong** for strength/power/hypertrophy | **Indirect** — supports the concurrent strength work (hybrid athlete); minor water-weight gain. Reasonable for the gym side. |
| **Everything else** (BCAAs, ketones, antioxidants megadoses, most "endurance blends")** | **Weak / unproven / sometimes counterproductive** (e.g., high-dose antioxidants may blunt adaptation) | Generally not recommended. |

Note: even "established" supplements show **1–25%** effects only under specific conditions, and third-party testing (contamination/doping risk) matters ([IOC consensus](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/28/2/article-p104.xml)).

**AI-coach implication:** recommend only caffeine (dosed, rehearsed) and optionally beetroot as performance aids; frame beta-alanine/bicarb as low-value for this event; note creatine only in the strength context; explicitly de-prioritize unproven products. Always "trial in training first."

## 6. Body-Composition & Weight Management

Endurance performance is partly about power-to-mass, so leanness helps — but **how** you get lean determines whether performance improves or collapses.

- **Goal is performance, not a scale number.** There is no single optimal weight; two same-height athletes can peak at different body compositions ([MyProCoach — race weight](https://www.myprocoach.net/blog/triathlon-race-weight/), [Scientific Triathlon EP#113](https://scientifictriathlon.com/tts113/)).
- **Never crash-diet in heavy training.** Severe restriction robs energy for workouts and recovery, degrades adaptation, and courts RED-S ([220 Triathlon — racing weight](https://www.220triathlon.com/training/nutrition-training/racing-weight-4-tips-for-combining-lean-with-power), [Third Coast Training](https://thirdcoasttraining.com/body-composition-face-off-triathletes-vs-cyclists-vs-runners/)).
- **Safe rate:** a deficit **≤~500 kcal/day** → ~0.25–0.5 kg/week, preserving muscle. Do body-comp work in **base**, not build/peak or race week.
- **Fuel the session regardless** of a weight goal — you can create a modest deficit at other times of day, not by under-fueling key/long workouts.
- **Health guardrails:** excessive leanness → reduced bone density, lost lean mass, disordered eating, menstrual dysfunction ([TrainingPeaks — body weight management](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/body-weight-management/)).

**AI-coach implication:** if the athlete sets a weight goal, only pursue it in base phases, cap the rate, keep training fueling intact, and hard-stop / escalate if any RED-S signal appears.

## 7. RED-S / Under-Fueling — Warning Signs a Coach Must Watch

**Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S)** results from **low energy availability** (intake too low for training demands) and harms both health and performance. It affects **male and female** athletes; endurance athletes are a high-risk group ([NSCA — RED-S](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/nsca-coach/relative-energy-deficiency-in-sport-reds-awareness-identification-and-management/), [PMC — RED-S qualitative case study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10155971/)).

**Warning signs (symptoms emerge gradually — monitor over time):**

- **Performance:** unexplained decline in speed/strength/endurance *despite* adequate training; stalled progress; delayed recovery; frequent illness or injury (esp. **stress fractures**).
- **Physical:** **loss of menstrual periods** (a major flag in female athletes), persistent fatigue, low libido / low testosterone signs (male), poor sleep, feeling cold, GI issues, low bone density.
- **Behavioral/psychological:** preoccupation with weight/food, restrictive eating, skipping meals or fueling on long sessions, withdrawal from social meals, compulsive extra exercise, mood/motivation decline, irritability.

**Coach response:** treat suspected RED-S as **medical** — increase energy availability, reduce training load, and **refer to a physician / sports dietitian**. Do not try to out-train or self-manage it ([NSCA](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/nsca-coach/relative-energy-deficiency-in-sport-reds-awareness-identification-and-management/), [Virtue Eating Disorder — return to sport](https://virtueeatingdisorder.com/blog/athletes-red-s/)).

**AI-coach implication:** monitor for the cluster — plateau/decline + low fueling reports + weight-loss intent + (for female athletes) cycle changes + injury/illness. If ≥2 flags co-occur, stop recommending any deficit, raise fueling, and **surface an explicit "please see a doctor/dietitian" message**. This is a safety boundary, not a coaching preference.

## 8. Practitioner Fueling Insights (from expert podcasts/videos)

The transcripts in [`youtube-transcripts/`](youtube-transcripts/) corroborate and sharpen the sections above with real coach/pro practice. Where they conflict, that is flagged.

**Race-day intake rates — the high-carb consensus (supports §3, methodology §9).** Multiple independent practitioners converge on ~90 g/h as the working target for age-group Ironman, higher for pros:
- Matt Dixon / Scott Tindle give a concrete threshold: females holding ~160+ W and males ~210–220+ W for 3+ hours benefit from **up to ~90 g/h**, even though absolute power is lower than a pro's — because the age-grouper is out there far longer ([pre-race fueling & GI](youtube-transcripts/purplepatch-matt-dixon-pre-race-fueling-gi-problems.md)).
- Pros run higher and individualize: Leon Chevalier won Ironman South Africa on **~108 g carbs/h, ~776 mg sodium/h, ~846 ml fluid/h**, and topped up salt tabs (250 mg each) on the run ([carrying race fuel](youtube-transcripts/gtn-leon-chevalier-how-pros-carry-race-fuel.md)); another debrief cites ~90 g/h, ~900 ml/h, ~700 mg sodium/h rated near-perfect execution ([what pros eat race day](youtube-transcripts/gtn-what-pro-triathletes-eat-race-day-ironman.md)).

**Separate hydration from calories (extends §4).** Andy Blow's core long-course principle: the three levers — fluid, sodium, carbohydrate — should be dialed **independently**. All-in-one sports drinks (~5–7% carb, ~500 mg/L sodium) suit short races, but over an Ironman fluid needs swing 2–3× with heat while calorie needs barely move, so no single drink is right at both extremes. Keep a constant tested carb intake (gels/solids) and throttle a near-zero-calorie electrolyte drink up/down with conditions ([strategic fueling](youtube-transcripts/purplepatch-matt-dixon-strategic-fueling-fluid-sodium-carbs.md)). This also puts a number on §4's "salty sweater" cue: average sweat sodium ~900–1000 mg/L (why a 1000 mg/L on-course drink targets the middle of the bell curve); >1500 mg/L sweaters fortify with capsules.

**GI-problem troubleshooting hierarchy (extends §3.2).** Dixon/Tindle's order for recurring gut distress: (1) rule out medical causes (celiac, IBS, FODMAP); (2) the most common culprit — not practicing race nutrition at race *intensity* and race *temperature*; (3) over-drinking fluid just to hit carb targets (split carbs into minimal fluid + solids instead); (4) carb intake oversized for actual output (a low-output athlete may only need 60–70 g/h from a single source). The gut adapts like a muscle — uncomfortable first exposure, easy by the third ([pre-race fueling & GI](youtube-transcripts/purplepatch-matt-dixon-pre-race-fueling-gi-problems.md)). "Taste fatigue" is real on long races — a neutral/mild flavor lets you keep drinking for hours ([optimizing nutrition](youtube-transcripts/purplepatch-matt-dixon-optimizing-triathlon-nutrition.md)).

**Carb-load timing (sharpens §2).** Dixon stresses the load starts **≥48 h out** (not the night before): ~6–8 g/kg at 48 h, 8–12 g/kg in the final 24 h, low-fibre/high-GI foods; the night-before pasta bowl is only ~100–150 g of a 500–700 g daily total, so front-load the day and finish dinner by ~18:30. Race-morning breakfast is a *rehearsed, automatic* ~2–3 g/kg (~150–200 g) eaten 2–3 h out ([pre-race fueling & GI](youtube-transcripts/purplepatch-matt-dixon-pre-race-fueling-gi-problems.md)). On-course maths tip: know your products' numbers (Maurten gel ~25 g; cola ~10 g/100 ml) and take caffeine *early* — its ~8 h half-life means saving it for the finish mainly wrecks post-race sleep ([optimizing nutrition](youtube-transcripts/purplepatch-matt-dixon-optimizing-triathlon-nutrition.md)).

**Gut trainability — the direct evidence (backs §3).** The Scientific Triathlon episode summarizes Jeukendrup's review plus the key 2019 study: two weeks of daily high-carb (90 g/h) gel intake cut gut discomfort 44%, eliminated severe symptoms, reduced malabsorption, and improved a 1-hour time trial 5%. It also notes daily-diet carb content up-regulates SGLT1 transporters — a direct argument *against* train-low-race-high ([training the gut](youtube-transcripts/scientific-triathlon-training-the-gut.md)).

**The low-carb / "metabolic efficiency" counter-view (tension with §1, §3 — hold the high-carb consensus).** Two sources argue for fat-adaptation and much lower race intake:
- Dan Plews advocates a "right fuel, right time" lower-carb approach (coached Chelsea Sodaro to Kona), warning against "fat phobia" (cutting carbs but under-eating fat) and stressing individualization via testing rather than copying elite 100+ g/h intakes ([Plews low-carb](youtube-transcripts/physical-performance-show-dan-plews-low-carb-fueling.md), [Plews EndureIQ physiology](youtube-transcripts/greg-bennett-show-dan-plews-endureiq-physiology.md)).
- The **UCAN/Seebohar webinar is sponsored** and pushes "metabolic efficiency" + SuperStarch with <100 kcal/h Ironman testimonials. Treat as one tool, not settled practice — its framing conflicts with the high-carb, gut-training consensus above; the defensible cross-cutting points are that chronic sugar spikes are metabolically costly and that blood-shunting makes cramming ever more carbs risk GI distress ([UCAN/Seebohar](youtube-transcripts/ucan-bob-seebohar-triathlon-nutrition-strategies.md)). **Net:** for a first-timer targeting a solid finish, the high-carb + gut-training path (§3) is the lower-risk default; fat-adaptation is an advanced, individually-tested option.

## Nutrition Rules Summary (If-Then)

- IF setting daily carbs → THEN scale to the day: rest 3–5, moderate 5–7, long/key 6–10, very heavy 8–12 g/kg; keep protein ~1.4–2.0 g/kg steady.
- IF an easy short day AND no RED-S risk → THEN "train-low" is optional; NEVER on key/long/quality days.
- IF race week → THEN carb-load **8–12 g/kg/day** starting 36–48 h out with low-fibre carb-dense foods; pre-warn ~1.5–2 kg normal water-weight gain; nothing new.
- IF building fueling tolerance → THEN start ~40–50 g/h, +5–10 g/h per step to race target, on race-like long sessions, with the exact race products (glucose:fructose 2:1) + matched fluid/sodium.
- IF personalizing hydration → THEN run a pre/post-weight sweat test (+ fluid in) for L/h; estimate sodium via lab/meter or "salty sweater" cue; rehearse the plan.
- IF recommending supplements → THEN caffeine (dosed, rehearsed) ± beetroot; treat beta-alanine/bicarb as low-value for Ironman; creatine only for the strength side; de-prioritize unproven products.
- IF the athlete wants to lose weight → THEN only in base, ≤500 kcal/day deficit, fuel all key sessions, stop if any RED-S flag.
- IF ≥2 RED-S flags (decline + under-fueling + weight-loss intent + cycle change / injury) → THEN raise energy availability, cut load, and refer to a physician/dietitian.

*Compiled July 2026. Daily-intake and supplement guidance are well-supported; Ironman-specific carb-load amounts and gut-training rates are coaching-practice extrapolations. RED-S handling is a safety boundary — refer out.*
